## Decision

A user can always tell exactly what a value is. Its type, unit and shape show on the card; wherever it's edited, the user sees the raw text they typed, and that text is what the document saves, with nothing hidden behind it. When typed text can't be read as a valid value of its type, it fails in a specific, visible way that names what went wrong. It is never guessed at, quietly reread as something else, or shown as a blank.

## Why

Excel lets a cell look like one thing and hold another: a rounded display over a longer number, `1.234,5` turning into text or a different number depending on the machine, a typo that reads as zero. Each of those is a wrong answer that looks right, and nobody finds it until it has spread. Saving the raw keystrokes on the surfaces that take them reaches huge swathes of the app, because every value downstream starts from that text — so it has to be the truth, read one way, and refused out loud when it can't be read. This is the editing half of [[B17]] typedValueModel: B17 keeps a value's type, unit and shape intact once it exists; this keeps it honest from the keystroke in.

## Consequences

- No saved value hides on a card, typed text saves as typed and reads one way everywhere, and a card the app doesn't know stays on the canvas as a visible stand-in rather than vanishing.
- Entry is stricter than Excel's: a value Excel would coerce into something plausible shows an error here instead.
- **Reopen if:** a surface has to save a derived value instead of what was typed (a paste whose raw text means nothing, say), or a failure that names its reason turns out less useful than a quiet best guess for some kind of entry.

## Alternatives considered

- **Generous entry** (Excel's way): interpret whatever is typed as best it can — dates, locale number forms, a formatted display over the stored value. It's faster for quick entry, but the user can no longer tell what a cell holds.
